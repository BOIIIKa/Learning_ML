<Step say="Discriminative: model the posterior directly and look for the boundary. Logistic regression from chapter four works this way.">Discriminative: learn $P(y\mid x)$ directly, i.e. draw the boundary</Step>

<Step say="Generative: first model each class, describing what its members usually look like, then reason backwards with Bayes' rule.">Generative: learn $p(x\mid y)$ and $P(y)$, then apply Bayes' rule</Step>

<Step effect="focus" say="Two questions: how are the two related, and which one is more reliable when data is scarce?">
<p class="big-q sm">How are they related? Which wins with little data?</p>
</Step>

<Slide lang="en" part="method" title="The triad of a generative classifier" say="Let's look at a generative classifier through the triad.">

<Step say="The model: the class prior times the class-conditional density, which is the joint distribution.">Model: $\pi_k \cdot p(x\mid k)$</Step>

<Step say="The strategy: maximize the joint likelihood. A discriminative model maximizes the conditional likelihood instead.">Strategy: maximize the joint likelihood $\prod_i \pi_{y_i}\,p(x_i\mid y_i)$</Step>

<Step say="The algorithm: the joint log-likelihood splits by class, each class fits its own distribution to its own data, and the priors are the class proportions. Counting and averaging are all it takes.">Algorithm: split by class, $\hat\pi_k = n_k/n$, count and average</Step>

<Step say="To predict, pick the class with the largest posterior, that is, the largest log-prior plus log-likelihood.">Predict: $\arg\max_k\ \log\pi_k + \log p(x\mid k)$</Step>

</Slide>

<Slide lang="en" part="method" layout="center" title="Three choices of class density" say="The choice of class-conditional density gives the method its name.">

<Step say="One mean and one covariance per class: quadratic discriminant analysis, QDA, with a conic boundary.">QDA: $\mathcal N(\mu_k, \Sigma_k)$ → quadratic boundary</Step>

<Step say="All classes share one covariance: linear discriminant analysis, LDA, with a straight boundary.">LDA: shared $\Sigma$ → linear boundary</Step>

<Step say="Features independent given the class: naive Bayes, whose parameter count grows only linearly with the dimension.">Naive Bayes: $p(x\mid k)=\prod_j p(x_j\mid k)$</Step>

<Step effect="focus" say="Going down, the assumptions get stronger and the parameters fewer: steadier estimates, but bias when the assumption is wrong.">
<p class="big-q sm">Stronger assumptions → less variance, more bias</p>
</Step>

</Slide>

<Slide lang="en" part="math" title="Why lines, and why curves" say="Write down each class's discriminant function.">

<Step say="After taking logs, the discriminant function contains a quadratic form.">

$$
\delta_k(x) = \log\pi_k - \tfrac12\log\det\Sigma_k - \tfrac12 (x-\mu_k)^\top\Sigma_k^{-1}(x-\mu_k)
$$

</Step>

<Step say="Subtracting two classes leaves a quadratic term: x transpose, times the difference of the inverse covariances, times x. With different covariances the boundary is a conic.">$\delta_1 - \delta_0 \ni -\tfrac12 x^\top(\Sigma_1^{-1}-\Sigma_0^{-1})x$ → quadratic</Step>

<Step say="With equal covariances the quadratic terms cancel, only linear terms remain, and the boundary is a straight line.">$\Sigma_0 = \Sigma_1$ ⇒ the quadratic terms cancel ⇒ a line</Step>

</Slide>

<Slide lang="en" part="math" title="The LDA posterior is a sigmoid" say="With a shared covariance, the log-odds is a linear function of x.">

<Step say="The weight vector is the inverse covariance times the difference of the means.">$w = \Sigma^{-1}(\mu_1 - \mu_0)$</Step>

<Step say="So the posterior is exactly a sigmoid: the same model family as logistic regression, estimated differently.">$P(y=1\mid x) = \sigma(w^\top x + b)$: the logistic family</Step>

<Step say="On the right, drag the spread ratio. At one, the quadratic coefficient a is zero and the posterior is a sigmoid; otherwise the wider class wins again in both tails.">Unequal spreads ⇒ $a \ne 0$ ⇒ a non-monotone posterior →</Step>

<div slot="visual"><PosteriorShape client:visible lang="en" /></div>

</Slide>

<Slide lang="en" part="math" title="Naive Bayes and Laplace smoothing" say="What about text? Use multinomial naive Bayes.">

<Step say="Each class has a set of word probabilities theta; the maximum-likelihood estimate is the word count over the total count.">$\hat\theta_{kj} = N_{kj}/N_k$</Step>

<Step say="The trouble: a word that never occurred in a class gets probability zero, and one word can veto the whole class.">One unseen word ⇒ the whole class gets probability 0</Step>

<Step say="Put a Dirichlet prior on theta and take the posterior mean to get the smoothed estimate. Alpha equal to one is Laplace smoothing.">

$$
\tilde\theta_{kj} = \frac{N_{kj} + \alpha}{N_k + \alpha V}
$$

</Step>

</Slide>

<Slide lang="en" part="cases" title="Worked example: sorting a message" say="Two urgent messages, three routine ones, and a new message: fever, pain, refill.">

<Step say="Without smoothing, refill never appeared in an urgent message, so the probability of urgent drops straight to zero.">$\alpha = 0$: $P(\text{urgent}) = 0$</Step>

<Step say="With Laplace smoothing, urgent scores one over two hundred sixteen and routine three over one thousand twenty-four, so the posterior of urgent is about zero point six one two.">$\alpha = 1$: $\frac{1/216}{1/216 + 3/1024} \approx 0.612$</Step>

<Step say="On the right, add words to the message yourself and see which way each word pushes the log-odds.">Each word's share of the log-odds →</Step>

<Slide lang="en" part="cases" title="Generative vs discriminative: sample efficiency" say="Naive Bayes and logistic regression are a pair: the same model family with different estimation strategies.">

<Step say="Naive Bayes converges fast, in about log d samples; logistic regression needs about d.">Generative: $O(\log d)$ samples · discriminative: $O(d)$</Step>

<Step say="But when independence fails, naive Bayes levels off at a higher error.">Wrong assumption ⇒ a higher limiting error for the generative model</Step>
