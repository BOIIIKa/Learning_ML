<Section lang="en" k="scenario">

A community clinic wants to screen for diabetes early. Everyone who comes in for a check-up has two measurements: **fasting glucose** and **BMI** (both standardized, so 0 is the population average). The clinic has records of past patients and knows which of them were later diagnosed. The doctor wants a rule: take the two measurements and decide whether to call the person back for further tests.

In the plane each patient is a point: orange for those later diagnosed, blue for those who were not. The most naive idea is to **draw a straight line that separates the two groups**. The algorithm below is called the **perceptron**, and its approach is almost crude: try a line, and whenever a point is on the wrong side, nudge the line in the direction that fixes it.

<PerceptronSteps client:visible lang="en" />

The replay raises three questions, and they map onto the three methods of this chapter:

1. **Is it guaranteed to stop?** If a straight line can separate the data, yes, and the number of mistakes is bounded (Novikoff's theorem).
2. **Is a yes/no answer enough?** The doctor would rather know **how high the risk is**, to decide whom to call first. That needs a probability, which brings in **logistic regression**.
3. **What about more than two classes?** Say "normal / prediabetes / diabetes". The **maximum-entropy principle** tells us that a multi-class probability model should take the softmax form.

</Section>

<Section lang="en" k="method">

### The perceptron: only mistakes are punished

<Box kind="definition" title="The perceptron's triad">

- **Model**: $f(x) = \mathrm{sign}(w\cdot x + b)$; the hypothesis space is every line in the plane (every hyperplane in higher dimensions).
- **Strategy**: the total distance from the misclassified points to the hyperplane. The distance from $x_i$ is $|w\cdot x_i+b|/\|w\|$, and for a misclassified point $-y_i(w\cdot x_i+b) > 0$. Dropping the factor $1/\|w\|$, which does not depend on the sum, gives the loss
  $$L(w, b) = -\sum_{i\in M} y_i\,(w\cdot x_i + b),$$
  where $M$ is the set of points currently misclassified (including those exactly on the boundary).
- **Algorithm**: stochastic gradient descent. $\nabla_w L = -\sum_{i\in M} y_ix_i$, using one misclassified point at a time:
  $$w \leftarrow w + \eta\,y_ix_i,\qquad b\leftarrow b+\eta\,y_i.$$

</Box>

The geometry of the update is plain to see: when a positive point is misclassified, add a bit of $x_i$ to $w$ so that $w\cdot x_i$ grows; for a negative point, do the opposite. If the data are linearly separable, $L$ eventually reaches 0, but **the solution is not unique**: a different start, step size or visiting order ends at a different line.

**The dual form.** Starting from $w = 0$, $b = 0$, the final $w$ is just a sum of terms $\eta y_ix_i$. If point $i$ triggered $n_i$ updates, let $\alpha_i = n_i\eta$; then

$$
w = \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_i y_i x_i,\qquad b = \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_i y_i,\qquad f(x) = \mathrm{sign}\Big(\sum_j \alpha_j y_j\,(x_j\cdot x) + b\Big).
$$

Checking whether $x_i$ is misclassified needs only the inner products $x_j\cdot x_i$, which can be precomputed as the **Gram matrix** $G = [x_i\cdot x_j]$. In Ch. 07 this "inner products only" property turns linear methods into kernel methods.

### Logistic regression: output a probability

The perceptron only tells the doctor yes or no. Logistic regression changes the model so that the **log-odds** are linear:

$$
\log\frac{P(Y=1\mid x)}{P(Y=0\mid x)} = w\cdot x + b
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
P(Y=1\mid x) = \sigma(w\cdot x+b) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-(w\cdot x + b)}}.
$$

The strategy is **maximum likelihood**: treat each label $t_i\in\{0,1\}$ as a Bernoulli variable and maximize $\prod_i p_i^{t_i}(1-p_i)^{1-t_i}$ with $p_i = \sigma(w\cdot x_i+b)$. Its negative logarithm is the **cross-entropy**:

$$
\ell(w, b) = -\sum_{i=1}^N\big[t_i\log p_i + (1-t_i)\log(1-p_i)\big].
$$

So "Bernoulli maximum likelihood" and "minimizing cross-entropy" are two names for the same thing. Gradient descent works as the algorithm, but **Newton's method** is more common, and here it has a name of its own: iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS).

<Box kind="insight" title="Why a probability beats yes/no">

With probabilities the doctor can rank patients by risk and, when resources are limited, call the most at-risk first. When a missed case and a false alarm cost different amounts, the threshold can move away from 0.5 (recall the Bayes classifier of Ch. 01: the best threshold depends on the costs and the priors). The perceptron's output has no such scale.

</Box>

### The maximum-entropy model: use what you know, assume nothing else

The maximum-entropy principle says: among all distributions that satisfy the known constraints, pick the one with the **largest entropy**, the one that adds no assumption beyond the constraints. For classification, take feature functions $f_k(x, y)$ and require each feature's expectation under the model to match its empirical expectation in the training data. The next section proves that the answer must be

$$
P_w(y\mid x) = \frac{\exp\big(\sum_k w_kf_k(x,y)\big)}{\sum_{y'}\exp\big(\sum_k w_kf_k(x,y')\big)}.
$$

With features $f_{c,j}(x,y) = x_j\,\mathbb 1[y = c]$ this is multinomial logistic regression (softmax); with two classes it reduces to $\sigma(w\cdot x+b)$ above. The classic solver is improved iterative scaling (IIS); today quasi-Newton methods (L-BFGS) are more common, and they are the default in scikit-learn's `LogisticRegression`.

</Section>

### Convergence of the perceptron: Novikoff's theorem

In augmented notation $\hat x = (x, 1)$, $\hat w = (w, b)$, the update reads $\hat w\leftarrow\hat w+\eta y_i\hat x_i$.

<Box kind="theorem" title="Novikoff's theorem">

Suppose some $\|\hat w_{\text{opt}}\| = 1$ satisfies $y_i\,\hat w_{\text{opt}}\cdot\hat x_i \ge \gamma > 0$ for every point, and let $R = \max_i\|\hat x_i\|$. Then the perceptron started from $\hat w_0 = 0$ makes at most

$$
k \le \Big(\frac{R}{\gamma}\Big)^2
$$

mistakes (updates) in total.

</Box>

**Proof.** Let the $k$-th update happen at the misclassified point $(x_i, y_i)$.

- **The inner product with the best direction grows by at least $\eta\gamma$ each time**: $\hat w_k\cdot\hat w_{\text{opt}} = \hat w_{k-1}\cdot\hat w_{\text{opt}} + \eta y_i\,\hat w_{\text{opt}}\cdot\hat x_i \ge \hat w_{k-1}\cdot\hat w_{\text{opt}} + \eta\gamma$, so by induction $\hat w_k\cdot\hat w_{\text{opt}}\ge k\eta\gamma$.
- **The length grows slowly**: $\|\hat w_k\|^2 = \|\hat w_{k-1}\|^2 + 2\eta y_i\,\hat w_{k-1}\cdot\hat x_i + \eta^2\|\hat x_i\|^2 \le \|\hat w_{k-1}\|^2 + \eta^2R^2$, because a misclassified point makes the middle term $\le 0$. By induction $\|\hat w_k\|^2\le k\eta^2R^2$.

By Cauchy–Schwarz, $k\eta\gamma \le \hat w_k\cdot\hat w_{\text{opt}}\le\|\hat w_k\| \le \sqrt k\,\eta R$, i.e. $k\le(R/\gamma)^2$. $\square$

Intuitively, the angle between $\hat w_k$ and the right direction closes at every step, and since the cosine of that angle cannot exceed 1, the updates cannot go on forever. A wider margin $\gamma$, i.e. data that are "easier to separate", gives a smaller bound. Conversely, **on data that are not linearly separable the perceptron never stops**; it keeps swinging back and forth.

### Gradient, Hessian and convexity of logistic regression

Write $z_i = w\cdot x_i+b$ and $p_i = \sigma(z_i)$. Using $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$, the derivative of one sample's cross-entropy with respect to $z_i$ is $p_i - t_i$ (exercise 2). In augmented notation $\theta = (w, b)$, with design matrix $\hat X$ whose $i$-th row is $\hat x_i^\top$,

$$
\nabla\ell(\theta) = \sum_i (p_i - t_i)\,\hat x_i = \hat X^\top(p - t),\qquad
\nabla^2\ell(\theta) = \sum_i p_i(1-p_i)\,\hat x_i\hat x_i^\top = \hat X^\top S\hat X,
$$

where $S = \mathrm{diag}\big(p_i(1-p_i)\big)$. For any $v$, $v^\top\hat X^\top S\hat X v = \sum_i p_i(1-p_i)(\hat x_i\cdot v)^2\ge 0$, so:

<Box kind="theorem" title="Convexity">

The negative log-likelihood $\ell(\theta)$ is convex (the log-likelihood is concave). If $\hat X$ has full column rank it is strictly convex, and a minimizer, if one exists, is unique.

</Box>

An easy trap: **on linearly separable data the maximum-likelihood estimate does not exist.** Scale $w$ up along a separating direction and every $p_i$ heads to the correct 0 or 1; the loss falls towards 0 but never reaches it. Stretch $w$ in the surface widget above and watch the surface get steeper. In practice we add an L2 penalty (scikit-learn's `C` is the inverse of its strength): the structural risk minimization of Ch. 01.

### Newton's method is iteratively reweighted least squares

Newton's method uses a second-order approximation: $\theta^{\text{new}} = \theta - (\hat X^\top S\hat X)^{-1}\hat X^\top(p-t)$. Folding $\theta$ in,

$$
\theta^{\text{new}} = (\hat X^\top S\hat X)^{-1}\hat X^\top S\,u,\qquad u = \hat X\theta + S^{-1}(t - p).
$$

This is weighted least squares of the **working response** $u$ on $\hat X$ with weights $S$ (recall the normal equations of Ch. 03). Each iteration updates the weights $S$ and the response $u$, hence "iteratively reweighted". Because the objective is smooth and convex, Newton's method converges **quadratically** near the optimum: the error is roughly squared at every step, and five or six steps usually reach machine precision.

### The dual of maximum entropy is softmax

Let $\tilde P(x, y)$ be the empirical distribution of the training data, with marginal $\tilde P(x)$. The maximum-entropy model solves

$$
\max_P\ H(P) = -\sum_x\tilde P(x)\sum_y P(y\mid x)\log P(y\mid x)
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad
\sum_{x,y}\tilde P(x)P(y\mid x)f_k(x,y) = \sum_{x,y}\tilde P(x,y)f_k(x,y),\ \ \sum_y P(y\mid x) = 1 .
$$

With multipliers $w_k$ for the feature constraints and $\beta_x$ for normalization, the Lagrangian is

$$
\mathcal L = H(P) + \sum_k w_k\Big(\sum_{x,y}\tilde P(x)P(y\mid x)f_k(x,y) - \mathbb E_{\tilde P}[f_k]\Big) + \sum_x\beta_x\Big(\sum_yP(y\mid x) - 1\Big).
$$

Setting the partial derivative with respect to $P(y\mid x)$ to zero gives $\tilde P(x)\big[-\log P(y\mid x) - 1 + \sum_kw_kf_k(x,y)\big] + \beta_x = 0$, so $P(y\mid x)\propto\exp\big(\sum_kw_kf_k(x,y)\big)$, and after normalizing

$$
P_w(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z_w(x)}\exp\Big(\sum_kw_kf_k(x,y)\Big),\qquad Z_w(x) = \sum_y\exp\Big(\sum_kw_kf_k(x,y)\Big).
$$

Substituting back into $\mathcal L$ gives the dual function

$$
\Psi(w) = \sum_{x,y}\tilde P(x,y)\sum_kw_kf_k(x,y) - \sum_x\tilde P(x)\log Z_w(x) = \sum_{x,y}\tilde P(x,y)\log P_w(y\mid x),
$$

which is exactly the **conditional log-likelihood**. Therefore:

<Box kind="theorem" title="Maximum entropy = maximum likelihood of a log-linear model">

The solution of the maximum-entropy problem has exponential-family (log-linear) form $P_w(y\mid x)\propto e^{w\cdot f(x,y)}$, and the dual problem for the best $w$ is maximum-likelihood estimation of that model. With $f_{c,j}(x,y) = x_j\mathbb 1[y=c]$ (plus $\mathbb 1[y=c]$ for the intercepts) we get softmax regression; with two classes, logistic regression.

</Box>

One consequence you can check is **moment matching**: the gradient vanishes at the maximum-likelihood solution, so $\sum_i p_i\hat x_i = \sum_i t_i\hat x_i$; the model's expected features match the data exactly. The die below draws this: the only constraint is the mean roll, and the maximum-entropy distribution is $p_k\propto e^{\lambda k}$.

### Example 1: the perceptron on three points

<Box kind="example" title="Primal and dual forms">

Positive points $x_1 = (2,2)$, $x_2 = (0,2)$; negative point $x_3 = (1,0)$. With $\eta = 1$, start from $w = 0$, $b = 0$ and check $x_1, x_2, x_3$ cyclically.

| Update | Misclassified point | $y_i(w\cdot x_i+b)$ when checked | $w$ after | $b$ after |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $x_1$ | $0$ | $(2, 2)$ | $1$ |
| 2 | $x_3$ | $-(2+1) = -3$ | $(1, 2)$ | $0$ |
| 3 | $x_3$ | $-(1+0) = -1$ | $(0, 2)$ | $-1$ |

Now $y_i(w\cdot x_i+b)$ is $3, 3, 1$, all positive, so the algorithm stops with the separating line $x^{(2)} = 0.5$.

**Dual form**: $\alpha = (1, 0, 2)$ and the Gram matrix is $G = \begin{pmatrix}8&4&2\\4&4&0\\2&0&1\end{pmatrix}$. Check: $w = 1\cdot(2,2) - 2\cdot(1,0) = (0,2)$ and $b = 1 - 2 = -1$. When $x_3$ is checked before the third update, $f(x_3) = \alpha_1y_1G_{13} + \alpha_3y_3G_{33} + b = 2 - 1 + 0 = 1$, so $y_3f(x_3) = -1\le 0$, exactly as in the primal form.

**Convergence bound**: $R = \|(2,2,1)\| = 3$. Taking the final solution $\hat w = (0,2,-1)/\sqrt5$ as reference, $\gamma = 1/\sqrt5$ and the bound is $(R/\gamma)^2 = 45$, while only 3 updates happened. The bound is loose, but it guarantees termination.

</Box>

### Example 2: Newton's method on five points

<Box kind="example" title="The first step already points the right way">

One-dimensional data $x = (-2,-1,0,1,2)$, labels $t = (0,1,0,1,1)$, parameters $\theta = (b, w)$.

**First step**: at $\theta = 0$, $p_i = \tfrac12$ and $\ell = 5\ln2\approx3.466$. The gradient is $\big(\sum(p_i-t_i),\ \sum x_i(p_i-t_i)\big) = (-0.5, -2)$ and the Hessian is $\tfrac14\begin{pmatrix}5&0\\0&10\end{pmatrix}$, so $\theta_1 = (0.5/1.25,\ 2/2.5) = (0.4, 0.8)$ with $\ell = 2.480$.

| Iteration $k$ | $b$ | $w$ | $\ell$ | $\|\nabla\ell\|$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 0 | 3.4657 | 2.06 |
| 1 | 0.4000 | 0.8000 | 2.4795 | 0.355 |
| 2 | 0.5797 | 1.0398 | 2.4236 | 0.052 |
| 3 | 0.6210 | 1.0886 | 2.42197 | $1.8\times10^{-3}$ |
| 4 | 0.6227 | 1.0904 | 2.42197 | $2.6\times10^{-6}$ |
| 5 | 0.6227 | 1.0904 | 2.42197 | $5\times10^{-12}$ |

The exponent of the gradient norm roughly doubles at every step: quadratic convergence. The final probabilities are $(0.174, 0.385, 0.651, 0.847, 0.943)$, which sum to $3.000$, exactly the number of positive labels: moment matching.

</Box>

### Example 3: a die with mean 4.5

The mean roll is known to be $\mu = 4.5$. The maximum-entropy solution is $p_k\propto e^{\lambda k}$; solving $\sum_k k\,p_k = 4.5$ gives $\lambda\approx0.371$ and

$$
p\approx(0.054,\ 0.079,\ 0.114,\ 0.165,\ 0.240,\ 0.347),\qquad H(p)\approx1.614 .
$$

For comparison, "uniform on 3 to 6" also has mean 4.5, but its entropy is only $\ln4\approx1.386$: it adds the assumption that 1 and 2 never come up, which the data never told us.

### Back to the clinic

The 60 patients in the widget overlap, so the perceptron would never stop on them. Logistic regression started at $\theta = 0$ gets the gradient norm from 28 down below $10^{-9}$ in 7 Newton steps; the mean cross-entropy drops from $\ln 2\approx0.693$ to $0.243$ and the training accuracy is $86.7\%$. More importantly, every patient gets a risk score, and the clinic can schedule follow-ups in that order.

### Extensions

- **Two routes to multiple classes**: one-vs-rest (train $K$ binary classifiers) and softmax (model all $K$ classes jointly). The latter's probabilities are normalized by construction; it is the answer maximum entropy gives.
- **Discriminative vs generative**: if each class is Gaussian with a shared covariance, the posterior from Bayes' rule also has the form $\sigma(w\cdot x+b)$. LDA and naive Bayes in Ch. 05 reach the same model from that side, but estimate its parameters differently.
- **From the perceptron to neural networks**: replace $\mathrm{sign}$ with a smooth activation and stack many perceptrons, and you have the multilayer perceptron of Ch. 14; the pairing of cross-entropy and gradient descent carries over unchanged.
- **Probabilities need calibration**: regularization and class imbalance can pull $\sigma(w\cdot x+b)$ away from the true frequencies. Check a reliability curve before using it as a risk score.
