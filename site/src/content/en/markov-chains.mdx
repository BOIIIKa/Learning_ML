The hospital statistician has built a Bayesian model of a new treatment's effect. The posterior is easy to write down (prior times likelihood), but its normalizing constant is an integral nobody can do, let alone the posterior mean or a credible interval. With more than a handful of parameters, grid integration is hopeless too.

### Markov chains and stationary distributions

<Box kind="definition" title="Markov chain">

A sequence of states $X_0, X_1, \dots$ with $P(X_{t+1} = j \mid X_t = i, X_{t-1}, \dots) = P_{ij}$: the next step depends only on the current state. The matrix $P$, whose rows sum to 1, is the **transition matrix**. A distribution $\pi$ with

$$
\pi^\top P = \pi^\top
$$

is **stationary**: once the chain is distributed as $\pi$, it stays that way forever.

</Box>

If the chain is **irreducible** (every state can reach every other) and **aperiodic**, the stationary distribution is unique, the distribution of $X_t$ converges to it from any start, and time averages converge: $\frac1T\sum_t f(X_t) \to \mathbb E_\pi f$ (the ergodic theorem). MCMC uses this theorem backwards: **fix $\pi$ first, then design a chain that has it as its stationary distribution.**

### Metropolis–Hastings

<Box kind="definition" title="The Metropolis–Hastings algorithm">

Target $\pi(x) \propto \tilde\pi(x)$ (only the unnormalized $\tilde\pi$ is needed), proposal $q(x' \mid x)$. At each step:

1. propose $x'$ from $q(\cdot \mid x)$;
2. accept it with probability $\displaystyle \alpha(x, x') = \min\!\left(1, \frac{\tilde\pi(x')\,q(x \mid x')}{\tilde\pi(x)\,q(x' \mid x)}\right)$ and move to $x'$; otherwise stay at $x$ (**and record $x$ again**).

With a symmetric proposal (say $x' = x + \varepsilon$, $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, s^2 I)$) the $q$'s cancel: this is the Metropolis algorithm.

</Box>

The normalizing constant cancels in the ratio, which is exactly why the method works for posteriors. The step size $s$ is the only knob: too small and nearly everything is accepted but the chain barely moves; too large and nearly everything is rejected. In practice random-walk Metropolis mixes best with acceptance around $0.2$–$0.5$ (the high-dimensional theoretical optimum is about $0.234$). The first stretch of samples, still influenced by the starting point (the **burn-in**), is thrown away.

**Gibbs sampling** is a special case of M–H: sample each coordinate in turn from its conditional $\pi(x_j \mid x_{-j})$; the acceptance probability is always 1. It is the method of choice when the conditionals are easy to sample (conjugate models).

### PageRank: a random walk on the web

A **random surfer** wanders between web pages: with probability $d$ they click a random out-link of the current page, and with probability $1 - d$ they jump to any page at all. The long-run share of time the surfer spends on each page is its **PageRank**:

<PageRankSurfer client:visible lang="en" />

In matrix form, with $M_{ij} = 1/\mathrm{out}(j)$ when $j$ links to $i$,

$$
r = \frac{1 - d}{n}\mathbf 1 + d\,M r.
$$

Pages without out-links (dangling nodes) are treated as linking to every page. $d = 0.85$ is the classic choice, and **power iteration**, plugging in repeatedly from the uniform distribution, finds $r$.

### Topic models: LDA

<Box kind="definition" title="Latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA)">

There are $K$ topics; topic $k$ is a distribution over the vocabulary, $\phi_k \sim \mathrm{Dir}(\beta)$, and document $d$ has topic proportions $\theta_d \sim \mathrm{Dir}(\alpha)$. Each word of a document is generated by first drawing a topic $z \sim \theta_d$ and then a word $w \sim \phi_z$.

**Inference**: given the words, find the posterior over topic assignments $z$. Integrating out $\theta$ and $\phi$ gives **collapsed Gibbs sampling** over each word's topic:

$$
P(z_i = k \mid z_{-i}, w) \ \propto\ (n_{dk}^{-i} + \alpha)\,\frac{n_{kw}^{-i} + \beta}{n_k^{-i} + V\beta}.
$$

$n_{dk}$ counts the words of document $d$ in topic $k$, $n_{kw}$ how often word $w$ is assigned to topic $k$, $n_k = \sum_w n_{kw}$, and the superscript $-i$ means "with the current word removed".

</Box>

The two factors match intuition: "topic $k$ is common in this document" × "this word is common in topic $k$". Drag the number of sweeps below and watch the topics separate out of a random assignment:

### Detailed balance ⇒ stationarity

<Box kind="theorem" title="Detailed balance">

If $\pi(x)\,T(x \to x') = \pi(x')\,T(x' \to x)$ for all states $x, x'$, then $\pi$ is a stationary distribution of the kernel $T$.

</Box>

**Proof**: sum over $x$:

$$
\sum_x \pi(x)\,T(x \to x') = \sum_x \pi(x')\,T(x' \to x) = \pi(x')\sum_x T(x' \to x) = \pi(x').
$$

Detailed balance says the probability flow between any two states is the same in both directions. It is stronger than stationarity, but much easier to check.

### M–H satisfies detailed balance

For $x' \ne x$ the kernel is $T(x \to x') = q(x' \mid x)\,\alpha(x, x')$, so

$$
\pi(x)\,q(x' \mid x)\,\alpha(x, x') = \min\big(\pi(x)\,q(x' \mid x),\ \pi(x')\,q(x \mid x')\big).
$$

The right-hand side is symmetric in $x$ and $x'$, so it also equals $\pi(x')\,q(x \mid x')\,\alpha(x', x)$: detailed balance holds and $\pi$ is stationary. (The $x' = x$ part is the same on both sides and needs no check.)

Gibbs sampling updates only coordinate $j$, with proposal $q(x' \mid x) = \pi(x_j' \mid x_{-j})$. The acceptance ratio is

$$
\frac{\pi(x')\,\pi(x_j \mid x_{-j})}{\pi(x)\,\pi(x_j' \mid x_{-j})} = \frac{\pi(x_j' \mid x_{-j})\,\pi(x_{-j})\,\pi(x_j \mid x_{-j})}{\pi(x_j \mid x_{-j})\,\pi(x_{-j})\,\pi(x_j' \mid x_{-j})} = 1,
$$

so a Gibbs move is always accepted.

### Existence and uniqueness of PageRank

Let $G = \frac{1 - d}{n}\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top + dM$ (with $M$ already fixed for dangling nodes, so its columns sum to 1). Every entry of $G$ is strictly positive and every column sums to 1.

<Box kind="theorem" title="Perron–Frobenius (positive matrices)">

A strictly positive column-stochastic matrix has eigenvalue $1$ with a unique positive eigenvector (normalized to sum to 1); every other eigenvalue has modulus strictly below 1.

</Box>

So the PageRank $r = Gr$ exists and is unique, and power iteration converges at rate $|\lambda_2|^t$; one can show $|\lambda_2| \le d$, so the closer $d$ is to 1, the slower the convergence. When $\mathbf 1^\top r = 1$ we have $\frac{1 - d}{n}\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top r = \frac{1 - d}{n}\mathbf 1$, which is the iteration in the method section.

### Where the collapsed Gibbs conditional comes from

$P(z_i = k \mid z_{-i}, w) \propto P(z_i = k, w_i \mid z_{-i}, w_{-i})$. Integrating out $\theta_d$, Dirichlet–multinomial conjugacy gives the predictive probability $\frac{n_{dk}^{-i} + \alpha}{n_d - 1 + K\alpha}$; integrating out $\phi_k$ gives $\frac{n_{kw}^{-i} + \beta}{n_k^{-i} + V\beta}$. The first denominator does not depend on $k$, and dropping it gives the formula of the method section. The whole inference only maintains three count tables.

</Section>

### Example 1: PageRank of a three-page web

<Box kind="example" title="A → B, C; B → C; C → A; d = 0.85">

$A$ has two out-links, $B$ and $C$ one each. The equations are

$$
r_A = 0.05 + 0.85\,r_C,\quad r_B = 0.05 + 0.425\,r_A,\quad r_C = 0.05 + 0.425\,r_A + 0.85\,r_B.
$$

The solution is $r = (686, 380, 703)/1769 \approx (0.388, 0.215, 0.397)$. $C$ ranks highest: it receives votes from both $A$ and $B$.

Power iteration from $(\frac13, \frac13, \frac13)$:

- step 1: $(0.333, 0.192, 0.475)$;
- step 2: $(0.454, 0.192, 0.355)$;
- step 3: $(0.351, 0.243, 0.406)$.

It converges while oscillating around the answer: the other two eigenvalues of $G$ are a complex pair of modulus $0.601$, so the error shrinks by about 40% per step.

</Box>

### Example 2: one Metropolis decision

<Box kind="example" title="A standard normal target">

Target $\pi(x) \propto e^{-x^2/2}$, current $x = 0$, symmetric proposal $x' = 1$. The acceptance probability is $\min(1, e^{-1/2}) \approx 0.607$: draw $u \sim U(0,1)$ and move if $u \lt 0.607$. The reverse proposal from $1$ to $0$ raises the density and is always accepted. "Always" one way and "61% of the time" the other way is exactly what makes the chain spend $e^{1/2}$ times as long at $0$ as at $1$: detailed balance.

</Box>

### Example 3: the topic of one word

<Box kind="example" title="LDA conditional, K = 2, V = 8, α = 0.5, β = 0.1">

A document contains "stock". With it removed, 3 of the document's words are in topic 1 and 1 in topic 2; across the corpus "stock" is assigned to topic 1 zero times and to topic 2 four times; the two topics hold 10 and 12 words in total.

$$
p_1 \propto 3.5 \times \frac{0.1}{10.8} \approx 0.0324,\qquad p_2 \propto 1.5 \times \frac{4.1}{12.8} \approx 0.4805.
$$

Normalizing gives $P(z = 2) \approx 0.937$: although the document leans towards topic 1, "stock" is so typical of topic 2 that it almost surely goes there.

</Box>

### Further

- **Convergence diagnostics**: run several chains from dispersed starts and compare between- and within-chain variance ($\hat R$); the effective sample size (ESS) discounts autocorrelation.
- **Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC)**: uses gradients and "momentum" to make long-distance proposals, far more efficient than a random walk in high dimension; NUTS picks the number of steps automatically and is the default sampler of Stan and PyMC.
- **Variational inference**: replaces sampling by optimization (the ELBO of chapter 11); online variational LDA handles millions of documents, and it is what scikit-learn's `LatentDirichletAllocation` uses.
- **Diffusion models and Langevin dynamics**: walking along $\nabla\log\pi$ with added noise is MCMC, and diffusion models learn exactly this "score" (Part V).
- **The spectrum of a Markov chain**: the spectral gap sets PageRank's convergence speed; the same idea drives spectral clustering and random-walk graph embeddings (DeepWalk).

</Section>
